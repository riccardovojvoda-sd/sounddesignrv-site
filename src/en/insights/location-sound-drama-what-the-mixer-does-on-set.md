On a drama set the sound is decided in a few minutes: the ones the production gives the sound mixer before shooting. What follows comes from my experience as a location sound mixer, often working alone, and describes what I can do on set and what I need from whoever organizes it. What can be recovered in post-production, and with which restoration tools, is a subject of its own.

## The ideal set

The set where I work best is the one where every department respects the others' roles. In practice: there is time to mic every actor and, if needed, to place microphones in the rooms. There is an open dialogue with the director of photography, because the boom has to stay where the camera cannot see it and where it casts no shadow in the frame. To manage that I learn the actors' movements and the camera moves, and I need a few extra minutes before the slate to do it.

## The difficult set

The difficult set is the one where neither the crew nor the actors have experience with location sound. The same things happen every time:

- actors sit on the wireless transmitter and break its connector;
- cables get pulled, or actors take their own mics off;
- there is no time for a sound test, and the fix is delegated to post-production.

## Alone or in a team

I often work alone: I record, I hold the boom and I listen in headphones to a mix of lavaliers and boom. What I cannot do is manage a live mix for the camera or correct a preamp problem while shooting. I can mic well with the boom and record everything properly, by feel and by trial.

With a team it is different: a mixer sits at the recorders and receivers and solves level and reception problems in real time, while the boom operator only takes care of the boom and hears in the headphones what the boom hears.

## Boom and lavalier

In post-production the main timbre almost always comes from the boom, the shotgun microphone. Lavaliers, the small body mics, provide proximity and are phase-aligned with the boom.

A lavalier works best at the center of the chest, 15-30 cm (6-12 in) from the mouth, or hidden in the hair or under a cap. Too close to the neck and you hear the throat vibrating in the low mids, too far and the body of the voice is lost. Clothes that rub against the capsule or cover it muffle the sound: the tricks for mounting it well deserve a guide of their own.

The boom has its own typical problem, the change of timbre when the microphone goes off axis or changes distance within the same line. In post it is reduced by blending the shotgun signal with the lavalier, in phase, to recover some timbre.

## Room, noise and location scouting

With wide shots you cannot get close with the boom, and in a reverberant room the lavalier alone is not enough. The ideal would be to have time and budget for a sound-focused location scout, and to decide together with the cinematography team how to treat the location acoustically for a drier sound.

For constant noises such as a refrigerator, an air conditioner or a generator, the rule on set is to switch them off or move them as far away as possible. In post-production constant background noise is manageable today without much trouble, but the less there is on set, the better.

## Three requests to the production

1. **Organize breaks and hot meals well.** A rested crew stays quiet and listens to instructions.
2. **Give time to mic** every scene and every actor properly.
3. **Leave a tail before the cut.** If there is traffic in the background, waiting for it to calm down, or for a few seconds to pass, allows a long, soft fade in post that hides the edits.

I add two things to agree on before shooting. People off camera should not repeat lines to the actor on camera, because every shot gets dirtied by audio that is not seen and in the edit it becomes hard to move from one take to another without reverb tails and overlaps. And the room tone, 30-40 seconds of absolute silence in the room, is what covers the gaps in the edit.

## The sound report

For whoever does audio post-production and for the editor it is essential to receive a [sound report](/en/insights/sound-report-location-sound-what-it-contains/) with what they need to find their way. It works if the production keeps track of scene names and if whoever claps the slate reads out the right information, precisely.

## In short

A few minutes of attention on set save hours of work afterwards. You do not need a crew of specialists: you need a sound mixer who is given time to do the job and a set where everyone knows what to avoid.
