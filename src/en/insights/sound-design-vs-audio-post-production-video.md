In video work, sound design and audio post-production are often used as synonyms, and sometimes they are: "sound design" sounds better and draws more interest from people outside the trade, "audio post-production" is the technical name. When the two terms mean different things, the difference is between the creative side of a video's sound and the technical side. Understanding it helps when writing a brief and reading a quote.

Here "sound design" has the meaning it has in video. It should not be confused with the other craft that goes by the same name: creating sounds from scratch or from samples, through synthesis and processing, for instruments, video games or sound libraries.

## Sound design: the creative side

Doing sound design means scoring a video with sound, creatively: building its sound world with effects, ambiences, foley and music, under an artistic direction. The video does more than show images, it can be heard: the material that bends, the room where someone is speaking, the move from one scene to the next.

The typical elements:

- **sound effects** (SFX): objects, machines, impacts, interfaces;
- **ambiences**: the background sound of a place, which gives space and continuity to the shots;
- **foley**: sounds recorded in sync with the picture, such as footsteps, clothes, hands, objects being handled;
- **transitions**: the sounds that go with cuts, titles and graphics;
- **textures and abstract sounds**, when the video tells an idea more than a scene;
- the **music choice**, when it is part of the artistic direction.

Sound design is not only for video: it is also done for installations, apps and radio programmes.

## Audio post-production: the technical side

Audio post-production works on material that already exists: interviews, production sound, voice-over, music, even sound design made by someone else. It puts it in order, cleans it up and brings it to a finished mix, ready for its destination.

It usually includes:

- **dialogue editing**: choosing the best takes, clean cuts, continuity from one shot to the next;
- **cleanup**: reducing noise, hum and reverb;
- **ADR**, re-recording in the studio the lines that were badly recorded on set, when needed;
- **music editing** to the cut: edits, endings and restarts lined up with scene changes;
- **mix**: the balance between voice, music and effects;
- **loudness and delivery**: the right level for each platform, explained in the [guide on what LUFS to deliver](/en/insights/what-lufs-to-deliver-a-video-instagram-youtube-tv-cinema/).

## Where they overlap

The line is not sharp. The mix also has a creative side and a matter of taste: deciding when the music makes room for a sound, or how much of an ambience to let through, is a storytelling choice as well as a technical one. The practical rule: when the artistic choices about sound are made by the person working on the audio, you are also buying sound design; when the job is to fix and mix material already chosen by others, it is post-production.

In video projects it pays not to stop at music and speech. Transition effects, foley and ambiences make the video live beyond the picture, even in an interview: in [Winemakers of Friuli](/en/projects/weldan/) the family's voices are the thread, and underneath there is a layer of sound design. At the opposite end, [The evolution of the species](/en/projects/lainox-naboo/) is all abstract sound design, built as a crescendo.

## Who does what

Several people often work on the audio of a video, and sometimes one person covers several roles:

- the **production sound mixer** records the voices on set;
- the **voice-over artist** records the narration;
- the **composer** writes the original music, when library music is not used;
- the **sound designer** builds effects, ambiences and foley;
- whoever does **audio post-production** edits, cleans up and mixes everything, and delivers.

## The most common misunderstanding

When an agency asks for "the sound design", it often means several things: the music choice, the voice mix, or all of the video's audio. To avoid quotes that cannot be compared, the brief should describe what is needed:

1. which audio material already exists (production sound, voice, music) and which needs to be created;
2. whether effects, ambiences and foley are needed, or only the voice and music mix;
3. where the video will be released (social, YouTube, TV, streaming platforms, cinema);
4. which files are needed at the end;
5. a reference piece, if there is one.

For the material to prepare for whoever works on the audio, see the [guide on what to deliver to your sound designer](/en/insights/what-to-deliver-to-your-sound-designer-aaf-handles-production-sound/).

## What you receive at the end

The basic delivery is the **master**, the finished mix, in the versions for the planned destinations: web, TV, platforms. On request you can also get:

- the **stems**, the mix split into dialogue, music and effects: summed together they give back the full mix. Netflix, for example, requires them for every original-language mix;
- the version **without voice** (M&E, music and effects), useful for adapting the video into other languages or replacing the voice-over;
- alternative versions, for example without music or with different cuts.

It is worth asking for them before work starts, even if they are not needed right away: preparing stems during the job is simple, rebuilding them months later much less so.
