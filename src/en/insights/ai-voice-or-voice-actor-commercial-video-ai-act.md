A synthetic voice can now be generated from a script in a few minutes, and for many videos it is a sensible choice. For others it is not. The difference lies less in the timbre, which the best services reproduce well, than in what the script asks of whoever reads it: the more interpretation it needs, the smaller the advantage of an AI voice.

## The two routes

- **AI voice**: you pick a voice in a speech synthesis service (ElevenLabs, Artlist and others), paste in the script and generate the reads, from the web interface or through the API.
- **Voice actor**: a person reads the script, from home or in a studio, starting from a brief.

The two routes often coexist in the same project: an AI voice as a temp track on the edit, a voice actor for the final version.

## Where an AI voice works well

- **Informational scripts** where the read needs to be clear more than expressive: tutorials, e-learning, internal videos, instructions.
- **Social content** with tight deadlines and a short life.
- **English** above all: it is the language on which the models are most mature. In Italian the results are good but less consistent, especially on cadence and accents.
- **Multilingual versions** of the same informational video, when the alternative would be a casting for each language.

## Where you hear the difference

The weak points of a synthetic voice are the ones that require understanding the script, not just pronouncing it:

- **emotion and subtext**: a line said with a smile, a pause that carries weight;
- **irony and comic timing**, where the timing of every syllable matters;
- **proper names and brands**, which the model may mispronounce or pronounce inconsistently;
- **dialogue between two voices**, the hardest thing to get right convincingly.

Then there is **file quality**. On the basic plans many services only export MP3, and the compression is audible: in the final mix, next to music and effects, a compressed voice loses presence. WAV is usually reserved for higher plans.

With experience of the tools and good editing, high-quality results are still achievable: you generate many variants, play with punctuation to push the model towards a different intention, then edit the best pieces together, sometimes syllable by syllable. It is a work of trial and collage. That is why the promise of speed holds for simple scripts: when the ambition rises, the editing time can cost more than a session with a voice actor.

## How the work changes

**With an AI voice** you start by choosing the voice, generate the reads, fix the parts that do not work and edit. Client revisions to the script are quick: a changed line is regenerated without calling anyone back.

**With a voice actor**, today the recording almost always happens remotely: the voice actor receives the brief, records from home and sends one or more reads, then the corrections are discussed. A studio session with live direction is the slowest route and, today, the rarer one, chosen when the performance is the heart of the project.

A practical detail about home recordings: every voice actor has their own setup and often applies processing to the file (compression, EQ, noise reduction). Whoever then mixes the video is better off also receiving the **clean, unprocessed recording**, so the sound of the voice is decided in the mix together with everything else.

## Which one to choose

- **TV and radio commercials, branded content, documentaries, emotional content**: a voice actor. That is where understanding of the script and performance make the difference, and you can hear it.
- **Social media, informational videos, e-learning, internal videos**: an AI voice is a real option, especially in English and when the script does not call for subtle acting.
- **In-between cases**: you can start with an AI voice as a temp track and decide on the edit whether it is enough or a voice actor is needed.

## Rights, licences and the AI Act

**The service licence.** Commercial use of an AI voice depends on the plan: with ElevenLabs, for example, content generated on the free plan cannot be used in a commercial project, while content generated on a paid plan can. It is worth checking the terms of the service you use before delivery.

**Cloned voices.** Cloning the voice of a real person requires their explicit consent, and the services require it in their terms of use. This also applies to the voice of a spokesperson or of a company employee.

**The AI Act.** From 2 August 2026 the transparency obligations of Article 50 of the European regulation on artificial intelligence apply:

- whoever **provides** the speech synthesis service must mark the generated audio in a machine-readable way, so that it can be detected as artificial (for systems already on the market before that date, the deadline is 2 December 2026);
- whoever **uses** the system must disclose **deep fakes**, meaning generated or manipulated content that resembles real people, places or events and could appear authentic. A cloned voice of an existing person falls here. In works that are evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional the obligation is lighter: it is enough to disclose it in a way that does not hamper the enjoyment of the work.

For a generic synthetic voice that imitates no one, the case is less clear-cut: the European Commission guidelines on transparency obligations help to assess it. When in doubt, disclosing it costs little. For a specific project the final assessment belongs to a lawyer.

## What you need to get started

The voice casting, human or AI, can be done by whoever handles the video's audio, together with editing, mixing and creative direction. A brief is enough to start:

1. what kind of voice: male or female, young or mature;
2. the mood: corporate, warm, energetic, ironic;
3. where the video will be released;
4. the reference video or, for radio, the programme or the product.
